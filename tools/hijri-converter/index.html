Umm al-Qura versus civil (tabular) Hijri dates

There is not one universal computational definition of an Islamic month. Two calendars may agree for weeks and then differ around a month boundary even though both display an Islamic year, month and day.

MethodHow it assigns monthsWhat it cannot establish
Umm al-QuraA calculated month sequence used by the Umm al-Qura calendar, supported in modern date-formatting software.Whether a crescent was actually sighted or accepted by a particular local authority.
Civil / tabular HijriA mathematical cycle with a defined leap-year rule and alternating month lengths adjusted within the cycle.A country’s observed new-moon date or the religious decision for Ramadan or Eid.

The converter deliberately names its two methods. The Unicode calendar identifiers distinguish islamic-umalqura from islamic-civil. The browser calendar documentation describes Umm al-Qura's supported 1300–1600 AH dataset and its fallback outside this range. We limit comparison to that AH span to avoid silently presenting fallback dates as Umm al-Qura dates.

Both outputs use the date-formatting calendar data on your device. If a device lacks an expected calendar implementation or uses different underlying data, its result may not match a printed calendar. A tabular date is particularly useful as a transparent arithmetic comparison, not as a sighting verdict.

Does the Hijri date change at midnight or sunset?

A Gregorian civil day normally turns over at local midnight. In Islamic reckoning, the religious date begins with the evening at sunset. That matters if you check the date late in the day, but a date-only converter does not know whether the Sun has set at your precise location.

For example, someone looking at a Gregorian Tuesday before Maghrib may refer to one numbered Hijri day; after Maghrib, the following Hijri day has begun in their religious day-counting convention, even though the Gregorian date is still Tuesday. Use the optional “next lunar day” switch only after establishing the local sunset boundary yourself.

Advancing a day in software does not simulate crescent visibility. Separately, local or national authorities may announce that the next lunar month starts on a different evening. The calendar used for worship should follow the decision recognised in your community.

The twelve Hijri months and important observances

Quran 9:36 establishes twelve months, with four accorded sacred status. Sahih al-Bukhari 4662 names those four as Muharram, Rajab, Dhul Qa'dah and Dhul Hijjah. Calendar calculations are useful for advance planning, while observance dates may require local confirmation.

Ramadan begins with the ninth lunar month and Eid al-Fitr with the tenth. Hajj rites and Eid al-Adha fall in Dhul Hijjah. The Ramadan timetable, Islamic events calendar and White Days planner are planning aids, not independent moon-sighting announcements.

Why local crescent announcements still matter

Quran 2:189 identifies lunar phases as a way to mark time. Sahih al-Bukhari 1909 connects Ramadan's start and end to the crescent and addresses overcast conditions. How communities evaluate and adopt sighting evidence is a matter for religious authorities, not a browser algorithm.

For travel, school dates, work schedules or booking arrangements, consider an estimated Gregorian date and allow for possible local differences. Where a date is tied to fasting, Eid prayer, Hajj or another obligation, check a trusted mosque or national council rather than relying only on the converter. Some communities make announcements close to the evening itself.

Frequently asked Hijri conversion questions

What is the Islamic date today?

The calculator starts with your device’s local Gregorian date and shows Umm al-Qura and civil/tabular Hijri labels. The day may change at your local sunset, and local moon-sighting announcements may differ.

Is Umm al-Qura the same as moon sighting?

No. It is a defined calculated calendar used for civil scheduling. It is not a live sighting bulletin for your mosque, country or location.

Why do my two Hijri results differ?

The tabular calendar follows a repeating arithmetic pattern. Umm al-Qura uses separately calculated month boundaries, so the same Gregorian date may have different numbered Hijri labels.

When does a new Hijri date begin?

In traditional Islamic day-counting, the new religious day begins at sunset rather than at civil midnight. The evening option is an illustration because this converter does not calculate or observe your exact sunset.

Can I use the result to announce Ramadan or Eid?

No. The software does not establish crescent visibility, local adoption of a sighting or an authoritative announcement. Confirm relevant dates with your recognised local religious authority.

Why does a month have 29 or 30 days?

Lunar months follow a cycle of about 29.5 days. Both supported calendar models allocate months of 29 or 30 whole dates; they may allocate them differently.

What happens if I enter 30 on a 29-day month?

The reverse converter shows no matching date instead of normalising the day to the following month. You can inspect the selected calendar’s month range and try day 29.

Can I convert an old historical Hijri date?

The comparison tool is intentionally limited to 1300–1600 AH. Umm al-Qura data is defined for this period; outside it, some software falls back to other calendar algorithms rather than historical sightings.

Does changing the calendar system change my religious obligations?

No. A numerical calendar comparison does not issue a legal or religious ruling. Follow qualified guidance for fasting, Hajj, the White Days and other observances.

Can I save or share the converted date?

Use Copy comparison or Download text summary. The exported note carries the calendar convention and a caveat. The converter does not save your date inputs.

Why might another website show a different date?

Check whether it uses Umm al-Qura, tabular calculation, an observational announcement, your timezone or an after-sunset convention. A one-day difference is not enough to identify the cause on its own.
